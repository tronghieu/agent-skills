# Fiction Studio

**Language:** [English](./README.md) | [Tiếng Việt](./README.vi.md) | [中文](./README.zh.md)

Develop a prose-fiction idea, draft, or series into author-led, craft-aware work that stays coherent from premise through revision.

```

```

```

```

## Why use it instead of an ordinary chatbot prompt?

An ordinary prompt can generate fluent prose, but often blends planning, drafting, criticism, and revision into one untraceable response. Fiction Studio separates those jobs, carries agreed decisions forward as artifacts, and pauses for the author's choices. Its named craft lenses make feedback specific: a structural problem is not treated as a line-edit, and a reader reaction is not mistaken for an instruction.

## Who it is for and when to use it

For new or experienced authors of novels, novellas, short stories, and prose series. Use it to:

- turn a premise into a genre-aware story plan;
- diagnose plot, character, setting, scene, prose, or dialogue problems;
- draft from an existing outline or revise an existing manuscript;
- obtain a focused beta-reader reaction and decide what feedback to act on;
- maintain continuity across a long project or multiple writing sessions.

For a short story, the process stays deliberately light. For a series, it can establish a shared world, genre promise, and series arc before each book follows its own pipeline.

## The studio method

Homer coordinates the project and returns control to the author between focused passes. The team’s lenses are: Aristotle (plot and pacing), Fyodor (character psychology), Tolkien (world and rules), Scheherazade (scenes and drafting), Oscar (dialogue), Max (developmental and line editing), Virginia (target-reader response), Borges (genre promises and comparable titles), and Bloom (optional literary critique).

For a new novel, work normally moves through:

1. Premise and genre promise
2. Outline — either a structural outline or a Snowflake-style expansion
3. Characters and world
4. Scene list and prose draft
5. Dialogue and developmental editing
6. Beta reading, revision triage, and final polish
7. Packaging: pitch material and a compiled manuscript

At each stage, the relevant specialist reads the earlier work, produces the next artifact, and checks back with you before the project advances. Quality gates cover structure, genre expectations, planted setups and payoffs, continuity, and prose; they support judgment rather than replacing it.

## What the experience feels like

Bring only the work you have. A bare idea can start at the premise; an outline, scene, or manuscript can enter at the needed pass. You will be offered a small set of meaningful options—such as raising stakes, changing a value turn, testing a character’s motivation, or trying a bolder alternative—rather than having the studio silently decide the story. You approve the direction and retain authorship throughout.

## Inputs, outputs, and working artifacts

Useful input includes your premise or text, genre and intended readership, tone, constraints, reference works, and decisions that must remain canon.

Depending on scope, the studio can build or update a premise, outline, character profiles, world bible, scene list, and manuscript chapters. Longer projects can also keep a machine-readable canon for names, facts, timelines, rules, and setups; beta notes; a prioritized revision plan; and a pitch kit containing a logline, blurb, synopses, comparable titles, metadata, and an optional query letter. These durable artifacts let a later session resume from the work, not vague recollection.

## Writers' Room: multi-persona party mode

The normal pipeline uses one focused persona at a time for production work such as drafting and editing. Party mode is different: it brings together three or four relevant voices when discovery benefits from productive disagreement—most often while shaping a premise, or at a major fork such as an ending, genre pivot, or stalled character.

The room frames one question, gathers distinct views, lets the perspectives test each other, then returns the choice to you. It closes by recording the direction, live alternatives, and open questions in the project. It is intentionally not used for line editing, drafting, or routine continuity work, where a single focused pass is clearer.

## Useful companion skills

- Use [Brainstorm Coach](../brainstorm-coach/README.md) before Fiction Studio when the idea itself is still wide open and you want to explore and prioritize possibilities before committing to a premise.
- Use [Deep Reader](../deep-reader/README.md) when your fiction is informed by long source texts—such as a public-domain work, historical source, or a body of comparable literature—and you need traceable notes before transforming that understanding into story choices.
- Use [Market Researcher](../market-researcher/README.md) when genre positioning, audience signals, or publishing-market assumptions need current, citation-backed desk research; bring the findings back to refine the genre promise and comparable titles.

## Limits

The studio supports prose fiction, not a complete publishing, legal, permissions, or professional sensitivity-review service. Genre conventions and beta reactions are lenses, not rules; the author decides what to keep, revise, or decline. Continuity records and quality checks catch many risks, but they cannot guarantee prose quality, cultural accuracy, market success, or factual accuracy in research-heavy fiction.
